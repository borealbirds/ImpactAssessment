Counterfactual Prediction of Bird Population Densities Under Isolated Sector Restoration
==========================================================================================

Overview
--------
We estimated the isolated effect of each industrial sector on bird population size by
constructing a sector-specific counterfactual: a simulated landscape identical to the
observed landscape except that biotic (vegetation) covariates at pixels belonging to a
given sector were replaced with model-predicted values representative of natural,
undisturbed conditions. Bird densities were re-predicted on this counterfactual landscape
using existing species distribution models, and the difference in predicted population size
between the observed and counterfactual landscapes was attributed to that sector.

Human Footprint Data and Sector Classification
-----------------------------------------------
Industrial footprint was characterised using continuous sector-specific human footprint
scores (Hirsh-Pearson layers; [citation]), available for 12 sectors: built, crop,
dam and associated reservoir, forestry harvest, mines, navigable water, night lights,
oil and gas, pasture, population density, rail, and roads. Each raster layer records,
at 1 km resolution, the degree of human influence attributable to that sector at each
pixel. An aggregate human footprint layer (CanHF) was used to classify pixels as
high-footprint (CanHF >= 1) or low-footprint (CanHF < 1); the latter threshold was
treated as representing intact, pre-industrial conditions for the purposes of this study.

All sector rasters and the aggregate CanHF layers were reprojected to the Canada Albers
equal-area projection (EPSG:5072) and resampled to 1 km x 1 km resolution to match the
covariate and bird density prediction grids.

Spatial Framework
-----------------
The study area encompasses the boreal and hemi-boreal regions of Canada covered by
National Models V5 [citation]. Spatial analyses were conducted at three nested scales:
Bird Conservation Region (BCR), Level 6 HydroBASINS subbasin, and individual sector
footprint (connected-pixel cluster; see below). Bird density models were fitted and
applied at the BCR level. Biotic covariate backfilling was performed at the subbasin
level using 674 Level 6 HydroBASINS subbasins [citation] that contained at least one
high-footprint pixel; data-sparse subbasins (below the 25th percentile of low-footprint
pixel count) were merged with spatially adjacent subbasins within the same BCR prior to
modelling. All spatial data were in the Canada Albers equal-area projection (EPSG:5072)
at 1 km resolution.

Stage 1 — Biotic Covariate Backfilling
----------------------------------------
For each subbasin, Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART; [citation]) were used to
predict what biotic (vegetation) conditions would have prevailed at high-footprint pixels
in the absence of human disturbance, by learning the relationship between abiotic and
biotic covariates at low-footprint pixels and extrapolating to high-footprint pixels.

Covariates were drawn from the National Models V5 covariate stack ([citation]; year 2020).
Abiotic predictors included climate normals, annual climate variables, topography, and
soil characteristics — variables considered insensitive to industrial disturbance.
Biotic covariates included 47 continuous variables (peatland cover, SCANFI forest
structural attributes including canopy closure, biomass, height, height CV, and species
composition; LandFire, ONRL, and ETH biomass and height products; RAPT canopy cover)
and 6 categorical land-cover variables (VLCE_1km, MODISLCC_1km, MODISLCC_5x5,
ABoVE_1km, SCANFI_1km, NLCD_1km).

Continuous biotic covariates were backfilled in a fixed hierarchical order (defined
a priori; see supplementary material), such that each covariate's BART model could use
all previously backfilled covariates as additional predictors. This cascading design
reflects the ecological dependency structure among vegetation attributes. Categorical
land-cover covariates were backfilled after all continuous covariates and were excluded
from the predictor set for continuous covariates to avoid non-numeric inputs.

For each subbasin and each biotic covariate b, the BART model was fitted as follows:

  - Training data: all low-footprint pixels within the subbasin with non-missing values
    for covariate b (CanHF < 1).
  - Predictors: abiotic covariates, all continuous biotic covariates preceding b in the
    hierarchy (replacing observed values with BART-backfilled values for subsequent
    iterations), and pixel centroid coordinates (latitude/longitude), centred and scaled
    to the subbasin mean and standard deviation to improve model performance.
  - A random 10% holdout was withheld from model fitting for out-of-sample evaluation.
  - Continuous covariates: Gaussian BART (gbart; BART R package [citation]) with a
    log(x+1) response transformation to ensure non-negative predictions. Model settings:
    50 trees (ntree = 50), 700 posterior draws (ndpost = 700), 300 burn-in samples
    (nskip = 300), shrinkage parameter k = 3, sparse variable selection prior
    (sparse = TRUE), and a prior on the residual SD anchored at the empirical SD of the
    log-transformed training response.
  - Categorical covariates: multinomial BART (mbart; BART R package [citation]).
  - Prediction at high-footprint pixels: all ndpost = 700 posterior draws were
    back-transformed (expm1) before summarising, yielding an empirical posterior mean and
    2.5th and 97.5th percentile predictions for each pixel. These three summaries
    (hereafter: mean, low, and high backfill scenarios) were retained to propagate
    uncertainty from the backfilling step into subsequent bird density predictions.

Outputs for each subbasin were saved as multi-layer rasters with one mean, q025, and q975
layer per continuous biotic covariate, and one layer per categorical covariate. Subbasins
with a constant or near-constant biotic covariate (<=2 unique values after log1p
transformation) were assigned the training-set mean for that covariate.

Stage 2 — Sector-Specific Counterfactual Covariate Stack Construction
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
For each industrial sector S, a sector mask was defined as the set of 1 km pixels
satisfying:

  sector_S_score > 0  AND  CanHF >= 1

where sector_S_score is the Hirsh-Pearson footprint score for sector S. This identifies
pixels with any measurable influence from sector S that are also classified as overall
high-footprint. Only these pixels received backfilled biotic covariate values in the
counterfactual; all other pixels — including high-footprint pixels belonging to other
sectors but not sector S — retained their observed covariate values. This construction
isolates the effect of sector S by holding all other industrial disturbance constant.

For each BCR, the counterfactual covariate stack was assembled as follows:

  1. Begin with the observed BCR-level covariate stack (National Models V5 [citation]).
  2. For each biotic covariate v, replace the observed value at sector S pixels with the
     BART-backfilled value:
       counterfactual[v, pixel p] = backfilled[v, p]  if p is in sector mask
                                  = observed[v, p]    otherwise
  3. Set all disturbance-class covariates (time-since-disturbance, fire history) to zero
     at all pixels, reflecting a no-disturbance counterfactual landscape.
  4. Repeat steps 2-3 separately for the mean, low (q025), and high (q975) backfill
     scenarios, producing three counterfactual covariate stacks per sector.

Subbasin-level backfill rasters were mosaiced to the BCR extent before step 2, with
pixels from adjacent subbasins merged using terra::merge() and all layers extended to a
common spatial extent (terra::extend()) to ensure a consistent raster footprint across
all subbasins within the BCR.

Stage 3 — Bird Density Re-prediction
--------------------------------------
Bird densities were predicted using the Boosted Regression Tree (BRT) models from
National Models V5 [citation]. For each species, a set of 32 bootstrap BRT models was
available per BCR, each fitted to a bootstrap replicate of the point-count survey data.
This ensemble characterises model uncertainty arising from sampling variability in the
bird survey data.

For each bootstrap model b (b = 1...32), bird density was predicted at every pixel in
the BCR under:
  (i)  the observed covariate stack (yielding observed density surface obs_b), and
  (ii) each of the three sector-specific counterfactual covariate stacks (yielding
       counterfactual density surfaces bf_b,mean, bf_b,low, and bf_b,high).

Predictions were made at the native 1 km resolution of the covariate stack. Density
values (birds km^-2) were multiplied by 100 to convert from the model's native unit
(birds per 100 km^-2; [citation]) prior to population summation.

Across the 32 bootstraps and three backfill scenarios (96 density surfaces per
prediction type), an incremental online algorithm (Welford's method) was used to compute
the mean and standard deviation of predicted density at each pixel without stacking all
surfaces simultaneously, reducing peak memory requirements.

Stage 4 — Population Aggregation and Impact Estimation
-------------------------------------------------------
Within each Level 6 subbasin, three population quantities were computed by summing
density x pixel area across relevant pixel sets (all pixels are 1 km^2):

  obs_total     : total observed bird population across the entire subbasin
  obs_on_S      : observed population at sector S pixels only
  bf_on_S       : counterfactual population at sector S pixels only (mean backfill scenario)

The counterfactual total subbasin population was:
  counterfactual_total = obs_total - obs_on_S + bf_on_S

Population quantities were summarised as means and standard deviations across the
32 x 3 = 96 prediction surfaces. Uncertainty in the SD reflects bootstrap variability
in the bird density models and uncertainty in the BART backfilling step (via the
low/high backfill scenarios).

The isolated impact of sector S on bird population size was expressed as a percentage
at four spatial scales:

  (1) BCR scale:
        pct_BCR = (bf_on_S - obs_on_S) / obs_total_BCR * 100
      where obs_total_BCR is the total observed population summed across all subbasins
      within the BCR.

  (2) Subbasin scale:
        pct_sub = (bf_on_S,sub - obs_on_S,sub) / obs_total_sub * 100
      computed separately for each subbasin, where obs_total_sub is the total observed
      population within that subbasin.

  (3) National scale:
        pct_national = sum_BCR(bf_on_S - obs_on_S) / sum_BCR(obs_total_BCR) * 100
      i.e. numerators summed across all BCRs and divided by the national observed total.

  (4) Footprint scale: individual spatially contiguous clusters of sector S pixels
      (connected components under 8-connectivity, i.e. pixels sharing at least one
      corner) were treated as discrete footprints and indexed 1...n within each BCR.
      For each footprint f:
        pct_f = (bf_on_f - obs_on_f) / obs_on_f * 100
      where the denominator is the observed population within footprint f itself.

Uncertainty Propagation
-----------------------
Standard deviations on population quantities were propagated using standard first-order
error propagation (delta method). For a ratio r = a / b:

  SD(r) = |r| * sqrt( (SD_a / a)^2 + (SD_b / b)^2 )

where SD_a and SD_b are the propagated standard deviations on the numerator and
denominator populations respectively. For the national-scale aggregation, BCRs were
treated as independent, so variances were summed across BCRs before taking the square
root.

Software
--------
All analyses were conducted in R [citation] using the terra package [citation] for
spatial operations, the BART package [citation] for Bayesian Additive Regression Trees,
and the gbm package [citation] for Boosted Regression Trees. Computationally intensive
steps (BART backfilling and bird density re-prediction) were executed as SLURM array
jobs on the Digital Research Alliance of Canada (formerly Compute Canada) cluster
infrastructure [citation], with one job per subbasin (backfilling) or one job per
species (re-prediction).
